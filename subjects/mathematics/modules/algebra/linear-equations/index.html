3.8 Linear Equations in Two Variables

Every solution
lives on the line

An equation like $x + y = 5$ has infinitely many solutions — and every single one of them is a point on one straight line.

03. A Pair of Equations

Where two lines cross

$x + y = 5$ and $2x - y = 4$ are two different lines. The single point where they cross satisfies both equations at once — that's the solution to the pair.

Solving this pair
1
Plot $x+y=5$: passes through $(0,5)$ and $(5,0)$.
2
Plot $2x-y=4$: passes through $(0,-4)$ and $(2,0)$.
3
Read off where the lines cross.
$(3, 2)$
4
Solution
$x = 3,\ y = 2$
Good to know

How many solutions does a pair have?

≠$\dfrac{a_1}{a_2} \neq \dfrac{b_1}{b_2}$ — lines cross once → exactly one (unique) solution
=$\dfrac{a_1}{a_2} = \dfrac{b_1}{b_2} = \dfrac{c_1}{c_2}$ — same line → infinitely many solutions
∥$\dfrac{a_1}{a_2} = \dfrac{b_1}{b_2} \neq \dfrac{c_1}{c_2}$ — parallel lines → no solution

Worked Example

Example
Is $(5, -1)$ a solution of $x - 2y = 7$ and $2x + 3y = 7$?
Concept used: substitute the point into both equations and check each result matches.
1
Check equation 1.
$x - 2y = 5 - 2(-1) = 5 + 2 = 7$ ✓
2
Check equation 2.
$2x + 3y = 2(5) + 3(-1) = 10 - 3 = 7$ ✓
3
Final answer
Both check out — $(5,-1)$ IS a solution of the pair.

Try Yourself

Exercise
Is $(3, 3)$ a solution of $2x - 5y - 2 = 0$ and $x + y - 6 = 0$?
Substitute $x=3, y=3$ into each equation and see if it equals 0.
1
$2(3) - 5(3) - 2 = 6 - 15 - 2 = -11 \neq 0$
Answer: No — the first equation already fails ($-11 \neq 0$), so $(3,3)$ is not a solution of the pair.

Linear equation in two variables

A linear equation in two variables has the form ax+by+c=0, where a,b,c are real numbers and a,b are not both zero. Each variable is first degree and the variables are not multiplied together.

Graphing a linear equation

Rewrite 2x+y=4 as y=4−2x, choose convenient x-values, calculate y-values, plot the ordered pairs, and join them. The textbook shows that all such points lie on one straight line and every point on the line is a solution.

Textbook Example 3.42
Draw graphs such as y=3x−1 and y=(2/3)x+3.
The textbook chooses convenient x-values (especially multiples of a denominator), creates a value table, plots the points, and joins them.

3.8.1 Simultaneous Linear Equations

A single linear equation has infinitely many ordered-pair solutions. A second equation can restrict the situation to a common solution. The textbook uses an eraser-and-pencil shopping situation to show why two equations are needed.

Textbook Example 3.43
Check whether (5,−1) satisfies x−2y=7 and 2x+3y=7.
Substitute the ordered pair into both equations. A simultaneous solution must satisfy both at the same time.
Progress CheckExamine graphically whether (3,3) solves 2x−5y−2=0 and x+y−6=0.

3.8.2 Graphical Method

Draw both lines on the same coordinate plane. Their point of intersection, if any, is the solution.

Textbook Example 3.44–3.47
Unique, no, and infinitely many graphical solutions; a rectangle word problem.
Intersecting lines give one solution; parallel distinct lines give no solution; coinciding lines give infinitely many solutions. The rectangle example converts two statements into equations and verifies the intersection.

Solving by Substitution

  1. Express one variable in terms of the other.
  2. Substitute into the second equation and solve.
  3. Back-substitute to get the remaining variable.
Textbook Example 3.48–3.49
Solve a pair by substitution and solve a reversed-digits word problem.
The textbook shows equation formation, substitution, and final verification—not just the numeric answer.

Solving by Elimination

  1. Multiply one or both equations so one variable has equal numerical coefficients.
  2. Add or subtract to eliminate that variable.
  3. Solve the resulting one-variable equation.
  4. Substitute back.
Textbook Example 3.50–3.51
Eliminate directly, then transform a nonlinear-looking pair using 1/x and 1/y.
The second example shows a clever substitution before elimination, reinforcing that method choice depends on equation structure.

Cross Multiplication Method

For a₁x+b₁y+c₁=0 and a₂x+b₂y+c₂=0, the textbook derives the coefficient formula and gives a visual cross pattern to remember it. Always first rewrite both equations in standard form.

Textbook Example 3.52–3.53
Solve pairs by cross multiplication and verify.
The examples emphasize correct signs for c₁,c₂ and clarify that the fraction-style notation used in the method does not justify division by zero.
