2.1–2.3 Rational & Irrational Numbers

Between any two
numbers, another hides

You already know Natural numbers $N$, Whole numbers $W$ and Integers $Z$. A rational number is a fraction $\frac{p}{q}$ of two integers, $q \ne 0$. But even after filling the number line with every rational number you can imagine, gaps remain — filled only by irrational numbers like $\sqrt2$.

2.2 Rational Numbers

$N \subset W \subset Z \subset Q$

Counting numbers $1,2,3,\dots$ are Natural numbers ($N$). Add zero and you get Whole numbers ($W = \{0,1,2,3,\dots\}$). Add negatives and you get Integers ($Z = \{\dots,-3,-2,-1,0,1,2,3,\dots\}$). A rational number is any number of the form $\frac{a}{b}$ where $a,b \in Z$ and $b \ne 0$ — we call the set of all such numbers $Q$. Since a fraction has many equivalent forms, $\frac13, \frac26, \frac{8}{24}$ all denote the same rational number.

$N = \{1,2,3,\dots\}$ $W = \{0,1,2,3,\dots\}$ $Z = \{\dots,-2,-1,0,1,2,\dots\}$ $Q = \left\{\frac{a}{b} : a,b \in Z,\ b\ne0\right\}$

2.2.1 Denseness Property of Rational Numbers

You can always squeeze in one more

For any two rational numbers $a > b$, their average $\frac{a+b}{2}$ is also rational, and it lies strictly between them: $a > \frac{a+b}{2} > b$. Since that average is itself a rational number, you can repeat the trick on the new, narrower gap — forever. This is the denseness property: between any two rational numbers there exist infinitely many rational numbers.

If $a=\frac{p}{q}$ and $b=\frac{r}{s}$, then $\dfrac{a+b}{2} = \dfrac{ps+qr}{2qs}$ is rational and lies between $a$ and $b$.

Worked Example

Example 2.1 (textbook)
Find any two rational numbers between $\frac12$ and $\frac23$.
Concept used: the arithmetic-mean (midpoint) trick, applied twice.
1
Average $\frac12$ and $\frac23$: $\frac12\left(\frac12+\frac23\right)=\frac12\cdot\frac{3+4}{6}=\frac{7}{12}$.
$\frac12 < \frac{7}{12} < \frac23$
2
Average $\frac12$ and $\frac{7}{12}$: $\frac12\left(\frac12+\frac{7}{12}\right)=\frac12\cdot\frac{6+7}{12}=\frac{13}{24}$.
$\frac12 < \frac{13}{24} < \frac{7}{12}$
3
Final answer
$\frac{7}{12}$ and $\frac{13}{24}$ both lie between $\frac12$ and $\frac23$ (there are infinitely many more).

2.3 Irrational Numbers

Not every point on the line is a fraction

Take an isosceles right triangle with both legs 1 unit. By Pythagoras, its hypotenuse is $\sqrt{1^2+1^2}=\sqrt2$. The Greeks proved $\sqrt2$ can never be written as $\frac{a}{b}$ for integers $a,b$ — it is irrational. An irrational number is one that cannot be expressed as an ordinary ratio of two integers.

$\sqrt2, \sqrt5, \sqrt7, 2\sqrt3$ $\pi$ (circumference ÷ diameter) $e$ (Euler's number) golden ratio $\varphi$

2.3.1 Irrational Numbers on the Number Line

Construct a square of side 1 unit. Its diagonal has length $\sqrt2$. Swing that diagonal down onto the number line with a compass centred at 0 — it marks $\sqrt2$ exactly to the right of 0, and $-\sqrt2$ to the left.

2.3.2 Decimal Representation of a Rational Number

Long division either stops, or repeats

Every rational number's decimal form comes from long division. Sometimes the remainder hits zero and the division simply ends — a terminating decimal. Watch it happen:

But the remainder doesn't always hit zero. Sometimes it starts repeating itself instead, forcing the same block of digits to repeat forever — a non-terminating recurring decimal.

Example 2.2 (textbook)
Represent as decimal form: (i) $\dfrac{-4}{11}$   (ii) $\dfrac{11}{75}$

A rational number's decimal expansion is either terminating, or non-terminating and recurring — and the converse is also true.

2.3.3 Period of Decimal

How many digits repeat?

The number of digits in the repeating block is called the length of the period of the decimal. For example $\frac{25}{7}=3.\overline{571428}$ has period length 6, and $\frac{27}{110}=0.2\overline{45}$ has period length 2.

Example 2.3 (textbook)
Express $\dfrac{1}{27}$ in recurring decimal form using the expansion of $\dfrac13$. Hence write $\dfrac{59}{27}$ in recurring decimal form.

$\frac13=0.\overline3$, so $\frac{1}{27}=\frac19\times\frac13=\frac19\times0.333\ldots=0.\overline{037}$. Then $\frac{59}{27}=2+\frac{5}{27}=2+\left(5\times\frac{1}{27}\right)=2.\overline{185}$.

2.3.4 Conversion of Terminating Decimals into Rational Numbers

Strip the decimal point, then simplify

$2.945 = 2 + \frac{9}{10}+\frac{4}{100}+\frac{5}{1000} = 2+\frac{945}{1000}=\frac{2945}{1000}=\frac{589}{200}$. In general, a terminating decimal with $n$ digits after the point equals (the digits, as a whole number) over $10^n$, then reduced.

Example 2.4 (textbook)
Convert to $\frac{p}{q}$ form: (i) $0.35$   (ii) $2.176$   (iii) $-0.0028$

Try the three textbook values above, then any terminating decimal you like.

(i) $0.35=\frac{35}{100}=\frac{7}{20}$   (ii) $2.176=\frac{2176}{1000}=\frac{272}{125}$   (iii) $-0.0028=\frac{-28}{10000}=\frac{-7}{2500}$

2.3.5 Conversion of Non-terminating Recurring Decimals into Rational Numbers

Multiply, subtract, and the repeating tail cancels

Let $x = 2.\overline4 = 2.4444\ldots$. Multiplying by 10 shifts the decimal point but leaves the same infinite tail of 4's: $10x = 24.4444\ldots$. Subtracting, $9x = 22.4444\ldots - 2.4444\ldots \Rightarrow 9x = 22 \Rightarrow x = \frac{22}{9}$.

Example 2.5 (textbook)
Convert to $\frac{p}{q}$ form: (i) $0.\overline3$   (ii) $2.\overline{124}$   (iii) $0.4\overline5$   (iv) $0.5\overline{68}$
Example 2.6 (textbook)
Without actual division, classify as terminating or non-terminating recurring: (i) $\frac{13}{64}$ (ii) $\frac{-71}{125}$ (iii) $\frac{43}{375}$ (iv) $\frac{31}{400}$
Rule: if $\frac{p}{q}$ can be written with denominator of the form $2^m5^n$ only, it terminates. Otherwise it is non-terminating recurring.
i
$64 = 2^6$ → only 2's.
$\frac{13}{64}$ terminates.
ii
$125 = 5^3$ → only 5's.
$\frac{-71}{125}$ terminates.
iii
$375 = 3 \times 5^3$ → has a factor of 3.
$\frac{43}{375}$ is non-terminating recurring.
iv
$400 = 2^4 \times 5^2$ → only 2's and 5's.
$\frac{31}{400}$ terminates.
Example 2.7 (textbook)
Verify that $1 = 0.\overline9$.
1
Let $x=0.\overline9=0.9999\ldots$
Multiply by 10: $10x=9.9999\ldots$
2
Subtract the first equation from the second.
$9x = 9 \Rightarrow x=1$
3
Conclusion
$0.\overline9 = 1$ — every terminating decimal can also be written with an endless tail of 9's.
Try Yourself
Convert $0.\overline{27}$ to $\frac{p}{q}$ form (period length 2 — same method as $2.\overline{124}$).
Let $x=0.\overline{27}$. Since the period is 2, multiply by 100 and subtract $x$.
$100x - x = 27 \Rightarrow 99x=27 \Rightarrow x=\frac{27}{99}=\frac{3}{11}$

2.3.6 Decimal Representation to Identify Irrational Numbers

Neither terminating, nor recurring

$\pi = 3.14159265358979\ldots$ never terminates and never repeats — no finite block of digits ever recurs. A number with a non-terminating, non-recurring decimal expansion is irrational. The square root of every positive non-perfect-square integer is irrational: $\sqrt2,\sqrt3,\sqrt5,\sqrt6,\sqrt7,\ldots$

Example 2.8 (textbook)
Find the decimal expansion of $\sqrt3$.
By long division of $3\div1$ after appending pairs of zeros (the square-root algorithm), $\sqrt3 = 1.7320508\ldots$ — non-terminating and non-recurring, so $\sqrt3$ is irrational.

Example 2.10 (textbook)
Find any 3 irrational numbers between $0.12$ and $0.13$.
Concept used: build non-terminating, non-recurring decimals that start $0.12\ldots$ by inserting a deliberately growing, never-repeating pattern.
1
Start each candidate with the digits $0.12$, so it's guaranteed to sit between $0.12$ and $0.13$.
2
Append a pattern of growing zero-blocks and 1's that never repeats, e.g. $0.12010010001\ldots$
3
Final answer
$0.12010010001\ldots,\ \ 0.12040040004\ldots,\ \ 0.12070070007\ldots$
Example 2.11 (textbook)
Give any two rational numbers lying between $0.5151151115\ldots$ and $0.5353353335\ldots$
Concept used: a terminating decimal placed strictly between the two given values is automatically rational.
1
Compare digit by digit: both numbers start $0.5\ldots$, then differ at the third digit (1 vs 3).
2
Final answer
$0.5152$ and $0.5352$ — both terminating, hence rational, and both fall strictly in the gap.
Example 2.12 (textbook)
If $a=2+\sqrt3,\ b=2-\sqrt3$; is $x=a+b$ rational or irrational? Is $y=a-b$ rational or irrational?
1
$x=a+b=(2+\sqrt3)+(2-\sqrt3)=4$
the $\sqrt3$ terms cancel — $x=4$ is rational.
2
$y=a-b=(2+\sqrt3)-(2-\sqrt3)=2\sqrt3$
an irrational number times a nonzero rational is irrational — $y$ is irrational.
3
Lesson
The sum, difference, product or quotient of two irrational numbers can be rational or irrational — it depends on the numbers.
Example 2.13 (textbook)
Represent $\sqrt{9.3}$ on a number line.
Construction: mark $A$, then $B$ with $AB=9.3$ units, then $C$ with $BC=1$ unit. Bisect $AC$ at $O$ and draw a semicircle of radius $OA$. Erect a perpendicular to $AB$ at $B$ meeting the semicircle at $D$; then $BD=\sqrt{9.3}$. Swing an arc of radius $BD$ centred at $B$ down onto the line to mark the point.
