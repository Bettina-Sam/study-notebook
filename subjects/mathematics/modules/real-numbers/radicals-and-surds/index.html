2.5 Radical Notation

$r^n = x \iff r = \sqrt[n]{x}$

Let $n$ be a positive integer and $r$ a real number. If $r^n = x$, then $r$ is called the $n$th root of $x$, written $r = \sqrt[n]{x}$. The symbol $\sqrt[n]{\phantom{x}}$ is a radical; $n$ is the index of the radical (earlier called the exponent); $x$ is the radicand.

When $n=2$: $r^2=x$, so $r=\sqrt[2]{x}$ — our familiar square root, usually written $\sqrt{x}$. When $n=3$: the cube root, $\sqrt[3]{x}$. For example $\sqrt[3]{8}=2$, since $2^3=8$.

Even index, both signs work — $(+2)\times(+2)=4$ and $(-2)\times(-2)=4$, so both $+2$ and $-2$ are square roots of 4. But it is incorrect to write $\sqrt{4}=\pm2$. By convention, when $n$ is even, $\sqrt[n]{x}$ always denotes the positive $n$th root, and the negative root is written $-\sqrt[n]{x}$. So $\sqrt{4}=2$ and $-\sqrt{4}=-2$. When $n$ is odd, there is exactly one real $n$th root for any $x$: $\sqrt[3]{8}=2$ and $\sqrt[5]{-32}=-2$.

2.5.1 Fractional Index

$\sqrt[n]{x}$ written as $x^{1/n}$

There is another way to represent roots — using fractional indices: we write $\sqrt[n]{x}$ as $x^{1/n}$. For example, $\sqrt[3]{64}$ is $64^{1/3}$ and $\sqrt{25}$ is $25^{1/2}$.

2.6 Surds

Roots you can't peel away

$\sqrt4$ resolves to 2, and $\sqrt{0.01}$ resolves to 0.1 — the radical symbol disappears. But $\sqrt{18}$ can't be resolved that way. A surd is an irrational root of a rational number: $\sqrt[n]{a}$ is a surd provided $n \in \mathbb{N}$, $n>1$, and $a$ is rational (but $\sqrt[n]{a}$ itself is irrational).

$\sqrt2$ is a surd — it is an irrational root of $x^2-2=0$, an equation with rational coefficients, and $\sqrt2 = 1.4142135\ldots$ is non-terminating, non-recurring.
$\sqrt[3]{3}$ (same as $3^{1/3}$) is a surd — an irrational root of $x^3-3=0$.
$\sqrt{\dfrac{1}{25}}=\dfrac15$ is not a surd (it simplifies to a rational number). Likewise $\sqrt[4]{\dfrac{16}{81}}=\dfrac23$ is not a surd.
$\pi$ is irrational, but it is not a surd — it is not a root of any equation with rational coefficients.

Why do surds matter? We usually take $\sqrt2\approx1.414$ and $\sqrt3\approx1.732$ for calculation, but $(1.414)^2=1.99936\ne2$ while $\sqrt2$ itself is the exact value. Engineers and scientists constructing bridges need that exactness — which is exactly what surds preserve.

2. Are all surds irrational numbers?
Yes. By definition a surd is an irrational root of a rational number — irrationality is built into what "surd" means.
3. Are all irrational numbers surds?
No. $\pi$ and $e$ are irrational but are not roots of any equation with rational coefficients, so they are not surds. Every surd is irrational, but not every irrational number is a surd.

2.6.1 Order of a Surd

The order of $\sqrt[n]{a}$ is $n$

The order of a surd is the index of the root being extracted. The order of $\sqrt[5]{99}$ is 5.

(i) Surds of the same order (equiradical surds) — same index. $\sqrt{x}, \sqrt{a^2}, \sqrt{2m}$ are all 2nd order (quadratic) surds; $\sqrt[3]{5}, \sqrt[3]{x-2}, (ab)^{1/3}$ are all 3rd order (cubic) surds.

(ii) Simplest form — a surd is in simplest form when written as a rational factor times an irrational factor, with (a) the smallest possible radical index, (b) no fraction under the radical, (c) no factor of the form $a^n$ left under an index-$n$ radical.
Example 2.18 (textbook)
Reduce $\sqrt3$, $\sqrt[4]{3}$, $\sqrt[3]{3}$ to surds of the same order.
1
LCM of the orders 2, 4, 3 is 12.
2
$\sqrt3=3^{1/2}=3^{6/12}=\sqrt[12]{3^6}=\sqrt[12]{729}$
3
$\sqrt[4]{3}=3^{1/4}=3^{3/12}=\sqrt[12]{3^3}=\sqrt[12]{27}$
4
$\sqrt[3]{3}=3^{1/3}=3^{4/12}=\sqrt[12]{3^4}=\sqrt[12]{81}$ — all now order 12.
Example 2.19 (textbook)
1. Express in simplest form: (i) $\sqrt8$   (ii) $\sqrt[3]{192}$    2. Show that $\sqrt[3]{7} > \sqrt[4]{5}$.
1
$\sqrt8=\sqrt{4\times2}=2\sqrt2$
2
$\sqrt[3]{192}=\sqrt[3]{4\times4\times4\times3}=4\sqrt[3]{3}$
3
LCM of orders 3, 4 is 12. $\sqrt[3]{7}=7^{4/12}=\sqrt[12]{7^4}=\sqrt[12]{2401}$
4
$\sqrt[4]{5}=5^{3/12}=\sqrt[12]{5^3}=\sqrt[12]{125}$
5
Since $\sqrt[12]{2401}>\sqrt[12]{125}$, therefore $\sqrt[3]{7} > \sqrt[4]{5}$.
(iii) Pure and Mixed Surds — a pure surd has coefficient 1 in simplest form (e.g. $\sqrt3, \sqrt[3]{6}, \sqrt[4]{7}$); a mixed surd has a coefficient other than 1 (e.g. $5\sqrt3, 2\sqrt[4]{5}, 3\sqrt[4]{6}$).

(iv) Simple and Compound Surds — a single-term surd is simple (e.g. $\sqrt3, 2\sqrt5$); an algebraic sum of two or more surds is a compound surd (e.g. $\sqrt5+3\sqrt2$, $3-2\sqrt7$).

(v) Binomial Surd — an algebraic sum/difference of exactly two terms, at least one a surd (e.g. $\dfrac12-\sqrt{19}$, $5+3\sqrt2$, $\sqrt3-2\sqrt7$).

2.6.2 Laws of Radicals

Four rules, for positive integers $m,n$ and positive rationals $a,b$

#Radical NotationIndex Notation
1$(\sqrt[n]{a})^n = a = \sqrt[n]{a^n}$$(a^{1/n})^n = a = (a^n)^{1/n}$
2$\sqrt[n]{a}\cdot\sqrt[n]{b} = \sqrt[n]{ab}$$a^{1/n}\cdot b^{1/n}=(ab)^{1/n}$
3$\sqrt[m]{\sqrt[n]{a}} = \sqrt[mn]{a} = \sqrt[n]{\sqrt[m]{a}}$$(a^{1/n})^{1/m}=a^{1/mn}=(a^{1/m})^{1/n}$
4$\dfrac{\sqrt[n]{a}}{\sqrt[n]{b}} = \sqrt[n]{\dfrac{a}{b}}$$\dfrac{a^{1/n}}{b^{1/n}}=\left(\dfrac{a}{b}\right)^{1/n}$
Example 2.21 (textbook)
Express each in simplest form and find its order, radicand and coefficient: (i) $\sqrt[3]{108}$   (ii) $(\sqrt[3]{1024})^{-2}$
1
$\sqrt[3]{108}=\sqrt[3]{27\times4}=\sqrt[3]{3^3}\cdot\sqrt[3]{4}$ (Law 2)
2
$=3\sqrt[3]{4}$ (Law 1). Order $=3$; radicand $=4$; coefficient $=3$.
3
$1024=2^{10}$, so $(\sqrt[3]{1024})^{-2}=\left(\sqrt[3]{2^{10}}\right)^{-2}=\left(\sqrt[3]{2^9}\cdot\sqrt[3]{2}\right)^{-2}$
4
$=\left(2^3\sqrt[3]{2}\right)^{-2}=(8\sqrt[3]{2})^{-2}=\dfrac{1}{64\cdot(\sqrt[3]{2})^2}=\dfrac{1}{64}\sqrt[3]{\dfrac14}$
5
Order $=3$; radicand $=\dfrac14$; coefficient $=\dfrac{1}{64}$.
Note — since $\sqrt5=\sqrt{25}$ and $\sqrt6=\sqrt{36}$, the numbers $\sqrt{26},\sqrt{27},\ldots,\sqrt{35}$ are all surds strictly between $\sqrt5$ and $\sqrt6$. Likewise $3\sqrt2=\sqrt{18}$ and $2\sqrt3=\sqrt{12}$, so $\sqrt{13},\sqrt{14},\sqrt{15},\sqrt{17}$ lie strictly between $2\sqrt3$ and $3\sqrt2$.

2.6.3 Four Basic Operations on Surds

(i) Addition and Subtraction

Like surds combine directly: $a\sqrt[n]{b} \pm c\sqrt[n]{b} = (a\pm c)\sqrt[n]{b}$, where $b>0$.
Example 2.22 (textbook)
(i) Add $3\sqrt7$ and $5\sqrt7$ — is the sum rational or irrational? (ii) Subtract $4\sqrt5$ from $7\sqrt5$ — rational or irrational?
1
$3\sqrt7+5\sqrt7=(3+5)\sqrt7=8\sqrt7$ — irrational.
2
$7\sqrt5-4\sqrt5=(7-4)\sqrt5=3\sqrt5$ — irrational.

$2\sqrt[3]{40}+3\sqrt[3]{625}-4\sqrt[3]{320}=2\sqrt[3]{8\cdot5}+3\sqrt[3]{125\cdot5}-4\sqrt[3]{64\cdot5}$
